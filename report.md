## 2. Problem

Predict household appliance electricity consumption, in watt-hours, one 10-minute
step ahead, from recent consumption, indoor climate sensors, outdoor weather and
calendar time. The task is a regression problem with strong temporal dependence:
consumption is spiky, autocorrelated, and dominated by a few switching events per
day. Six linear modelling strategies are compared under one evaluation protocol,
and the question is which strategy is best *for this dataset*, answered empirically
rather than assumed from the literature.

## 3. Data and split

- Source: UCI Appliances Energy Prediction dataset, one household, 19,735 observations at 10-minute intervals.
- Period: 2016-01-11 17:00:00 to 2016-05-27 18:00:00.
- Cleaning removed nothing: no missing values, no duplicate or invalid timestamps.
- Chronological 80/20 split: 15,788 training rows and 3,947 holdout rows. The split is never shuffled.
- `rv1` and `rv2` are random columns shipped with the dataset and are never used.
  `lights` is excluded so the target describes appliance use, not total household load.
- Because the models predict one step ahead, the design uses 
  15,788 training origins and 3,947 holdout origins; the origin for the first holdout target is the last
  training observation, which is legitimately available at prediction time.

## 4. Evaluation protocol

1. **Target.** `Appliances` at *t+1*, one 10-minute step ahead.
2. **Information set.** Every feature is measured at *t* or earlier. Nothing from the
   target time, and nothing from the holdout period, is used to build features.
3. **No holdout leakage.** Standardisation statistics and all hyperparameters are
   estimated from training rows only.
4. **Time-aware tuning.** Hyperparameters are chosen by forward-chaining (blocked)
   cross-validation inside the training period. Random k-fold is never used, because
   in an autocorrelated series it leaks information across the fold boundary.
5. **Identical rows.** Every model, including the references, is scored on the same
   holdout timestamps, so differences are attributable to the method.
6. **Determinism.** Closed-form solves and deterministically initialised coordinate
   descent; no random seed is needed and reruns reproduce exactly.

### Discussion

- **The field is tight.** 0.783 Wh of RMSE separates the best and worst of the six. On a holdout whose daily peaks exceed 1000 Wh, that is not a meaningful difference in behaviour; it is a difference in the last few percent of squared error.
- **RMSE and MAE disagree.** Polynomial ridge wins RMSE; Distributed lag (OLS) wins MAE. Squared error is dominated by the handful of large switching events, absolute error is not. Reporting one number and hiding the other would be a choice, not a fact.
- **The naive benchmark is strong.** Persistence reaches MAE 26.651 Wh. The RMSE winner is *worse* on MAE (27.448 Wh), so on typical error there is little to choose between copying the last reading and fitting a degree-2 penalised regression. What the models buy is protection against the largest misses.
- **The L1 family pays for its sparsity.** Lasso and elastic net keep only a few of the 29 channels and finish at the back of the six on both metrics, so the sparsity is a real cost here, not a free lunch.
- **The distributed-lag hypothesis was rejected.** Lag length was selected from {0, 1, 2, 3, 6, 12} by blocked CV and the winner was zero lags, so past outdoor weather adds nothing once the current readings and indoor sensors are present.
- **Information beats methodology.** OLS, base + six appliance lags (reference), a plain OLS with six appliance lags, beats all six tuned models. The six share a library containing only `appl_lag1`; the reference sees six lags. The largest single gain available in this project is more consumption history, not a more sophisticated penalty.
- **Not comparable with the published benchmark.** Lu, Lu & Kassymova (2026) report ridge at RMSE 84.94 Wh and R2 0.126 on this dataset under chronological validation. Our ridge reaches RMSE 61.243 Wh and R2 0.5475, but their feature set has no lagged target term while ours includes `appl_lag1`, and persistence alone already reaches MAE 26.65 Wh here. The gap reflects the information set, not a better estimator.

## 9. Limitations

- One household over about 4.5 months. No claim generalises to other homes, climates or seasons.
- A single chronological holdout block. A rolling-origin evaluation over several folds would
  give a tighter ranking, and the close spread of the six makes this the main methodological
  weakness of the comparison.
- Errors are heavy-tailed and the paired tests treat them as independent, which they are not;
  the tests are reported as indicative only.
- Coefficients are associations on a standardised scale, not causal effects.
- The comparison against published work is not like-for-like, for the reason given above.
